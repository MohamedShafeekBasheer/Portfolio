When you click **Create site** in SharePoint, the first thing it asks is whether you want a **team site** or a **communication site**. Most people pick one at random. It matters, because changing your mind later is awkward.

One question settles it:

> **Are most people going to *work* in this site, or *read* it?**

## Pick a team site when people work together

A team site is for a group that shares files and edits things together: a project, a department, a committee.

- It comes with a **Microsoft 365 group**, so it can have a Microsoft Teams team, a shared mailbox and calendar, and a Planner board.
- Everyone you add as a **member** can add and edit content by default.
- Good examples: *Finance team*, *Website redesign project*, *HR policies (draft)*.

If your team already uses Microsoft Teams, you already have one: every team has a SharePoint team site behind it, where the **Files** tab lives.

## Pick a communication site when a few people write for many readers

A communication site is for publishing: a few authors, lots of readers.

- It has **no Microsoft 365 group**, so no Teams team and no group mailbox.
- Most people get **read-only** access, and only the owners and a handful of editors can change it.
- It's built for good-looking pages and news posts.
- Good examples: *Company intranet home page*, *IT help and how-tos*, *HR: policies and benefits*.

## The quick test

| If… | Choose |
|---|---|
| A group edits documents together | Team site |
| It needs a Teams team or shared calendar | Team site |
| A few people publish, everyone else reads | Communication site |
| It's the intranet or a department's "front door" | Communication site |

A common, good pattern is to have both: a **team site** where the HR team works on drafts, and a **communication site** where the finished policies are published for everyone.

## One more tip

Pick a clear site name and web address at the start. The address (`/sites/finance-team`) is hard to change later, and people will bookmark it.
